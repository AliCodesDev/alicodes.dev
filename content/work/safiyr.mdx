A patient sits down with a doctor they have never met. They have fifteen minutes. Somewhere in the patient's memory, and somewhere in a folder of PDFs on their phone, is the information the clinician needs. Most of it never makes it across the desk.

Safiyr was infrastructure for that gap. Before a consultation, the patient tells their medical story to a conversational interface called Naia. The system reconciles that spoken narrative against the documents they upload, and hands the clinician a brief where **every single clinical fact is traceable to the sentence it came from** — a patient's own words, or a specific line in a specific document.

I was the sole engineer. I designed and built it over five months: the event-sourced backend, the encryption layer, the extraction and reconciliation pipeline, and both frontends.

It is shut down. I will get to why.

---

## The constraint that shaped everything

Two regulatory facts governed every technical decision:

**GDPR Article 9.** Health data is a special category. Processing it demands an explicit legal basis, real consent machinery, and genuine erasure — not a `deleted_at` column.

**The Medical Device Regulation.** Software that diagnoses, triages, or recommends treatment is a medical device in the EU. Certification is a multi-year, multi-hundred-thousand-euro undertaking.

Most teams treat these as a compliance checklist bolted on at the end. I treated the second one as an architectural requirement: Safiyr had to be **non-MDR by construction** — not "we told the model not to diagnose," but *the system has nowhere to put a diagnosis.*

That single decision is the most interesting engineering in the project.

---

## Non-MDR by construction

The system extracts clinical facts from free text using an LLM. An LLM will happily tell you that a patient's symptoms suggest a condition. The moment it does, the product becomes a regulated medical device.

So I built four reinforcing layers, none of them sufficient alone:

**Layer 1 — the system prompt.** French-language instructions explicitly forbidding diagnosis, severity classification, triage, recommendation, prescription, and assessment.

**Layer 2 — the tool-use schema.** This is the structural control, and it does the real work. The extraction schema's `entity_type` enum is hard-coded to five discriminators: `medication`, `condition`, `allergy`, `lab_result`, `vital_sign`. There is no `severity` field. No `urgency`. No `recommendation`, `assessment`, `triage_priority`, or `diagnosis`. **The model cannot return a diagnostic judgement because the schema has nowhere to put one.**

**Layer 3 — validation.** Pydantic discriminated unions with `extra='forbid'`, plus a runtime allowlist filter. The allowlist derives from the entity subclasses' own discriminators rather than a duplicated list, so schema drift fails loudly at import time instead of quietly at runtime.

**Layer 4 — the rendering boundary.** The clinician UI mirrors the data shape one-to-one. No counts, no ordering by importance, no visual emphasis that could imply severity. This is enforced at the design-token layer: a `[data-theme="clinical"]` surface that structurally cannot express urgency, because the tokens for it do not exist.

I rejected two tempting alternatives. **Prompt-only** fails because prompts drift across model upgrades — a new model version shifts behaviour subtly at the boundary, and a regulatory-line failure could go undetected for months. The schema constraint doesn't drift. **Token-stream regex filtering** solves the wrong problem: it is whack-a-mole against a generative model, and it would also strip legitimate verbatim quotes from a document that happen to contain a prohibited word.

I also rejected using a second model as a safety classifier. The regulatory line is too sharp for a probabilistic gate. You do not defend a legal boundary with something that is right 97% of the time.

## Provenance: verbatim or nothing

The clinician's trust in the brief is the entire product. If a fact might be a paraphrase or a hallucination, the brief is worse than useless — it is a liability.

So provenance is structural. Every extracted fact carries an envelope recording where it came from. Facts marked as direct quotes are **enforced verbatim against the source text**, not merely requested to be. The `extraction_method` field carries the trust signal, so a clinician reading the brief can tell at a glance whether they are looking at the patient's exact words or a structural inference.

Narrative extraction runs on the current conversational turn only. This costs real recall — a patient saying "I stopped taking it last year" two turns after naming the medication produces a fact I miss. I accepted that. Cross-turn coreference resolution would have meant the model reconstructing what the patient *probably* meant, and a reconstructed quote is not a quote. Missing a fact is recoverable. A fabricated verbatim citation in a medical brief is not.

## Reconciliation: surface conflicts, never resolve them

The patient says they take 5mg. The discharge summary says 10mg. What should the system do?

Nothing. It flags both, shows the clinician each source, and gets out of the way. Deciding which is correct is a clinical judgement — that is precisely the line that turns software into a medical device.

The matcher dispatches per entity type, and the scope tightens as clinical risk rises:

| Entity type | Matching |
|---|---|
| Medication | Full matching — dose, frequency, route |
| Condition, allergy | Agreement-only — flag agreement, never assert conflict |
| Lab result, vital sign | Disabled in V1 |

Labs and vitals are disabled because meaningful comparison requires understanding reference ranges and temporal context — which is interpretation, which is the line again. Better to ship nothing than to ship a comparison that implies a judgement.

One detail I am quietly pleased with: the conflicts projection contains **no PHI at all**. Conflict rows are purely structural — pointers to two envelopes and the field they disagree on. The sensitive content stays encrypted in the envelopes. A leaked conflicts table reveals that a disagreement exists, never what it was about.

## Encryption and real erasure

Every piece of PHI is encrypted at the application layer with AES-GCM under envelope encryption: a per-patient key-encryption key in AWS KMS wrapping per-record data keys. The GCM additional authenticated data binds `patient_id`, `event_id`, and `codec_version` at fixed length, so a ciphertext cannot be transplanted between patients or events without failing authentication.

Erasure is where most systems quietly cheat. GDPR requires actual erasure; most implementations set a flag. Safiyr uses **two reinforcing layers**:

**Layer 1 — application read-block.** Every decode consults the patient's key record *before issuing any KMS call* and raises immediately if destruction is scheduled. This makes erasure effective the instant it is requested. A clinician opening a still-valid brief link five minutes after a patient withdraws gets a hard refusal, not data.

**Layer 2 — key destruction.** KMS `ScheduleKeyDeletion`, and after the mandatory seven-day window the key is destroyed. Every data key that ever depended on it — live rows, restored backups, exported logs — becomes mathematically unrecoverable.

Neither layer works alone. Layer 1 doesn't protect a backup taken before erasure: restore it and the key still exists. Layer 2 is not effective for seven days, during which the application could keep decrypting. Together they close both gaps.

The cost is one indexed lookup per decode, on a small table Postgres keeps hot. I profiled it. At V1 volumes it is background noise.

## Why I wrote the event store myself

I considered the `eventsourcing` library and EventStoreDB, and rejected both.

The event store is the load-bearing layer of the entire system, and I was a solo engineer building toward an HDS audit. In that audit I need to defend every line of the concurrency control, the idempotency semantics, and the projection-trigger ordering. Frameworks abstract away exactly the semantics that matter most under that kind of scrutiny.

The patterns I actually needed — optimistic version checking, idempotent replay, snapshots — are small and thoroughly documented. Owning them cost less than negotiating with a framework's opinions. And the door stays open: if a framework becomes compelling later, that is an evaluation, not a forced rewrite.

The same instinct shows up in the brief tokens. Not JWT — a custom binary format, HMAC-SHA256 over a fixed-length 70-byte body with a 32-byte tag and a format-version byte. JWT carries an enormous algorithm-confusion attack surface for a token that needs to encode four fields and be unforgeable.

## How it was built

215 numbered architectural decisions, each a dated record with the reasoning, the alternatives considered, and why they lost. Supersessions and amendments tracked in-line, so the log reads as an argument that evolved rather than a pile of assertions. 24 specs, written before implementation.

The discipline was not ceremony. Working alone across five months, the decision log was how I stayed honest with my past self — and it is the reason I could write this page from memory-free source rather than reconstruction.

Everything ran through `ruff`, `mypy --strict`, and `pytest` locally and in CI, with pre-commit and pre-push hooks. TypeScript ran `strict` plus `noUncheckedIndexedAccess` and `exactOptionalPropertyTypes`. Ten named non-MDR property invariants were pinned by tests, so the regulatory boundary could not regress silently.

Naia ran on Claude Sonnet 5 with thinking disabled to hold the voice latency budget. The voice path was a cascade — VAD, STT, LLM, TTS — rather than a speech-to-speech model, because a cascade gives you an inspectable transcript at the boundary, and provenance requires text you can quote.

## Why it stopped

The engineering worked. The business did not.

French health data hosting requires HDS certification. The reimbursement pathways, the procurement channels, the question of who actually pays for a tool like this — all of it turned out to be substantially harder than I had assessed going in. The technical risk I had planned for. The regulatory-commercial risk I underestimated.

In August 2026 I decommissioned the infrastructure. Scaleway instance, AWS resources, KMS key — all torn down, and the key crypto-shredded, which is the same erasure guarantee I built for patients applied to the project itself.

I stopped paying thirty euros a month for a system with no route to a customer. That was the right call and I would make it faster next time.

## What I would do differently

**Validate the commercial path before building the hard parts.** I could have learned what I learned about French reimbursement in three weeks of conversations. I learned it in five months, having already built the encryption layer.

**Jurisdiction is an architectural input.** I treated "EU healthcare" as one market. It is not. The same product has meaningfully different viability in France, Germany, and Spain, and that should have been a decision record before line one.

**The non-MDR architecture was right regardless.** Of everything here, it is the piece I would keep unchanged. Encoding a regulatory boundary into a schema — so that violating it requires changing the type system rather than merely misbehaving — is a technique I will use again for any constraint that actually matters.
